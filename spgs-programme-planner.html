A small, unfamiliar show in a house museum that runs excellent private views. Churchill as an amateur painter is a conversation nobody at this table has had, and the Wallace is a room these guests will be pleased to be in after hours.

The best-kept secret within walking distance of the school: an intact Arts and Crafts interior, Morris's own circle, left as its owner had it. Talk — the flower-arranging idea, with a spine Gluck and Constance Spry

The cheapest enrichment you have

Talks, and who could give them

A talk costs a room and a case of wine, fits the weeks no exhibition covers, and is the only category where the school itself is the unique asset.

First, do not duplicate Development. The Development office already runs an In Conversation series — Dame Julia Hoggett and Sonya Branch are on at school this Thursday, 10 September, 6.30pm, free ticket, drinks after. That series has careers, the City and leadership covered, and it evidently lands significant names. The white space for Arts & Enrichment is skills and stories rather than CVs: evenings people leave able to do something, or knowing something they could not have read.

1  The school's own history

No other institution can run these evenings, which is exactly what makes them worth doing. All four are documented fact, not school legend.

Musicon site

Holst wrote The Planets in this building

Gustav Holst was the school's first Director of Music, 1905–34. The school built him a soundproof studio; he wrote The Planets in it, and composed the St Paul's Suite and the Brook Green Suite for these pupils. The Holst Room is still here.

The evening: a Holst scholar or the Director of Music in the Holst Room, with current pupils playing the St Paul's Suite — the piece written for girls sitting where they sit. Then drinks. This is the single most distinctive event available to you and it costs almost nothing.

Flowersanswers your brief

Gluck and Constance Spry

Gluck — Hannah Gluckstein, here from 1911 — was a painter who invented her own picture frame and painted stylised flower pieces. In 1932 she met Constance Spry, who invented modern British flower arranging. They became lovers, toured gardens together, and Gluck painted Spry's arrangements; Spry used Gluck's Chromatic in her first book.

The evening: the flower-arranging idea with a spine. An art historian on the two of them, then a Spry-method demonstration, and everyone leaves with an arrangement. The Garden Museum has run Spry exhibitions and is publicly hunting for the lost Chromatic — an obvious partner, and a good reason for them to say yes.

Sciencefor the daughters

The woman who designed the Reptile House

Joan Beauchamp Procter, here from 1916, became the first female Curator of Reptiles at London Zoo. She designed the 1927 Reptile House herself — differentiated heating, aquarium-principle lighting, visitors in the dark — and wrote the early accounts of Komodo dragon behaviour. She was dead at 34.

The evening: a ZSL curator on Procter, ideally paired with an out-of-hours walk through the building she designed. Unexpected, rigorous, and the strongest thing on this page for a daughter who is not especially moved by another gallery.

Stage & pageon site

Three Paulinas who shaped how the century looked

Celia Johnson, whose face carries Brief Encounter and whose name is on your theatre. Dodie Smith, who wrote I Capture the Castle and The Hundred and One Dalmatians. Jocelyn Herbert, who changed what post-war British stage design looked like.

The evening: one talk, three women, in the Celia Johnson Theatre, with clips. A theatre historian can carry this; a designer working today would be better.

3  The parent body — run an audit, do not guess

This is the highest-yield and cheapest source, and the mechanism matters more than any list I can write. You cannot guess which parent restores clocks. Ask one question in the parents' newsletter, and ask it the right way:

“What could you teach twenty people to do in ninety minutes?”

Not “what do you do?” — job titles will get you another panel on private equity. The banker who bakes is a better evening than the banker.

People answer that question badly from a blank page and well from a list. Seed it with these:

  • Arranging flowers for a table you actually eat at
  • A week of dinners, planned in twenty minutes
  • Knife skills, properly
  • Bread, or cheese, or chocolate — led by someone obsessive
  • Tasting wine without pretending
  • How to prune almost anything
  • Buying art you will still like in ten years
  • What actually happens at an auction
  • Photographing your own children properly
  • What an interior designer does to a room in an hour
  • Calligraphy, bookbinding, marbling
  • How a film gets financed
  • Reading a balance sheet, for the non-finance parents
  • Negotiating — for the daughters as much as the parents
  • Talking to a teenager about money
  • Writing a condolence letter, and a difficult email
  • Sleep, perimenopause, and what nobody schedules
  • Packing, and travelling light

4  Formats that beat “a talk”

Demonstration with a take-home. People remember what they made. An arrangement, a loaf, a print — the object does the marketing for the next one.
Two Paulinas in conversation. Development's format, already proven here, and easier to fill than a single speaker. A good chair is worth more than a second name.
Walk and talk. The Hammersmith river walk — Morris at Kelmscott House, Emery Walker's house, the Dove — led by a parent who knows it. Free, and on the school's doorstep.
In someone's house. A parent's kitchen, garden or collection, twenty people. Scarcity does the work that a budget otherwise has to.
Daytime for pupils, evening for parents. One speaker, two sittings. Halves the cost per head and makes it a school event rather than a social — which also makes it easier to ask.
Supper club. A talk over dinner for twenty rather than a lecture to a hundred. The right shape for anyone who would be wasted on a lectern.

Where these go in the year. Talks are the only inventory you can place freely, so use them on the weeks the exhibition calendar leaves empty: mid-January, once the Christmas programme has cleared and nothing new has opened; late February, after half term; and late April, in the first fortnight of summer term before exams start. Those three windows are visible as gaps on the board above.
